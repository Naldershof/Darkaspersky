# Ending Cinematic — Age of Fracture (Default)

## The Mending

> (No spoken narration. The cinematic is visual and scored.)

[Visual: The Tarnished stands before the shattered Elden Ring. Its fragments float in the air, golden and broken. The Tarnished reaches out and begins to reassemble the Ring — piece by piece, rune by rune, fitting the shards back together.]

[Visual: The Elden Ring reforms. It is whole, but the scars of its shattering are visible — golden seams where the fractures were, like kintsugi. The Ring is mended but not restored to its original state. It carries its history in its repaired form.]

[Visual: The Tarnished sits upon the throne. Runes flow from the mended Ring into the Erdtree, and the tree's light strengthens, but not to its former glory. The golden light is dimmer, more uncertain. The Age of Fracture begins — an era defined not by the Golden Order's perfection but by its imperfect repair.]

## Interpretation

The Age of Fracture is the default ending, requiring no special Mending Rune. The Tarnished simply mends the Elden Ring as-is, without modifying its fundamental nature. The result is a continuation of the existing order — the Erdtree still stands, Grace still flows — but the Shattering's damage is permanent. The Ring is held together but remains fractured in essence.

This ending represents the most conservative choice: restoration without transformation. The Golden Order continues, but weakened, scarred, and aware of its own fragility. The Tarnished becomes Elden Lord of a diminished kingdom, ruling over a world that knows its gods can break.

The name "Age of Fracture" is telling. Not "Age of Restoration" or "Age of Renewal" — fracture. The defining characteristic of this new age is the break that preceded it. The Shattering is not undone; it becomes the foundation of what comes next.

[Visual: The final shot shows the Erdtree from a distance, glowing gold but with visible dark patches — areas where the light does not fully reach. The world continues. It is not healed.]
